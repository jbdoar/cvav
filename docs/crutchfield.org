* Discrete model
The paper uses biased intensity I in [-1,1], with -1 black and +1 white.
Equation (1) combines storage and an optical image transform:

\[ I_{n+1}(\mathbf{x}) = L I_n(\mathbf{x}) + s f I_n(b R\mathbf{x}), \]
\[ R = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi & \sin\phi\\-\sin\phi & \cos\phi\end{pmatrix}. \]

L controls persistence, f is optical gain, and s is +1 or -1 for luminance
inversion. The paper specifies zero intensity outside its bounded image domain.
In biased coordinates that is neutral gray.

Equation (2) adds Gaussian spatial coupling:

\[ \langle I_n(\mathbf{x})\rangle_x =
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} d\mathbf{y}\,I_n(\mathbf{y})
\exp\left[-\frac{|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}|^2}{2(\sigma_f+\sigma_v)^2}\right]. \]

Its printed kernel has no normalization factor. Equation (3) is

\[ I_{n+1}(\mathbf{x}) = L I_n(\mathbf{x})
+ L'\langle I_n(\mathbf{x})\rangle_x + s f I_n(bR\mathbf{x}). \]

Equation (4) further replaces the first image by a weighted history
\(\langle I_n\rangle_\tau = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} I_{n-i} L^i\).
Later sections discuss color coupling and a reaction-diffusion model.

* Implemented approximation
Experiment 001 implements equation (3), with a normalized discrete Gaussian,
bilinear resampling, and additions for live input and bounded display values:

\[ I_{n+1}=\operatorname{clip}_{[-1,1]}\left(
L I_n + d\,G_\sigma * I_n + s f W_{z,\theta}(I_n)
+ a C_n + \beta\right). \]

C is grayscale webcam input mapped to [-1,1]. Its coefficient a is additive
forcing, not an alpha blend. Set it to zero after introducing a seed to explore
an autonomous feedback loop. Beta is brightness. Clipping supplies a simple
saturating nonlinearity motivated by the appendix; it is not a calibrated
vidicon response. This is not a reproduction of all the paper's simulations.

The diffusion slider is d (analogous to L'), and blur sets sigma in pixels.
A normalized Gaussian separates kernel width from gain; its boundary is reflected.
The warp uses zero outside the frame, as in the paper. Zoom z is *visible image
magnification*: source coordinates are divided by z, so b=1/z in the paper's
sampling expression. Positive angles rotate the displayed image counterclockwise;
OpenCV uses downward-positive pixel y. Rotation is in pixel coordinates, without
stretching a rectangular image to the paper's normalized square.

We use one previous image, not equation (4)'s full history, and monochrome rather
than the color-coupled model. Each processed frame is one discrete iteration.
The target is 30 iterations/second; slower capture/computation changes wall-clock
speed, not the map coefficients. Headless runs execute as fast as possible.

* Things to try
- Wave a hand with the defaults, then lower camera input to zero.
- Try gain near 100%, zoom just above 1, and a small rotation for spirals.
- Toggle inversion and vary persistence for alternating light/dark structures.
- Increase blur and diffusion to change spatial coupling.
- Strong gain or brightness can saturate the image. Clear with =r=, restore
  controls with =d=, or reduce gain and reintroduce the camera.

* Experiment 002: color feedback
Run with =--experiment color=. The state and camera input now have three
channels. OpenCV stores them in BGR order; capture, processing, display, and
PNG output all retain that order.

The paper's equation (5) extends scalar intensity to an RGB vector and uses
matrices to couple channels in the storage and diffusion terms. This experiment
is inspired by that linear coupling idea, but places the adjustable coupling
in the transformed feedback path. It retains the one-frame approximation of
experiment 001 rather than implementing equation (5)'s full temporal history.

In RGB notation, let P(R,G,B)=(B,R,G). Positive color cycle moves red into green,
green into blue, and blue into red; negative values reverse the cycle. With
c in [-1,1], the mixing matrix is

\[ M_c=(1-|c|)\,\mathrm{Id}+|c|\,P_{\operatorname{sign}(c)}. \]

After mixing, saturation t acts as a linear color transform:
\(S_t(v)=\ell(v)\mathbf{1}+t(v-\ell(v)\mathbf{1})\), where
\(\ell(v)=0.299v_R+0.587v_G+0.114v_B\).
The feedback term becomes \(sf S_t(M_c W(I_n))\); storage, diffusion, camera
injection, brightness, and final clipping are otherwise unchanged and act
per channel. These operations work in biased webcam/display values, not
radiometrically linear light. Saturation is an artistic extension.

At cycle=0 and saturation=1, the channels evolve independently under the
monochrome model. At saturation=0 only the transformed feedback is grayscale;
the live camera still supplies color. Inversion complements all three channels.
The default cycle is +12% per iteration. Try reducing camera input after moving
a colorful object, then reverse the cycle to change the sequence of colors in
its trails. Strong coupling tends to mix colors toward gray; increased
saturation can restore chroma, while high gain can drive channels to clipping.
